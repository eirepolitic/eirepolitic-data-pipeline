# 8 July 2026 — Planning for Constitutional Change Bill 2026

## Formal vote

- **Type:** substantive Private Members' Bill, Second Stage (resumed).
- **Result:** Tá 69; Níl 79; Staon 0.
- **Margin:** 10.
- **Outcome:** Bill defeated at Second Stage.

## What was being voted on

The Bill proposed a statutory planning process for possible constitutional change / Irish reunification. Its central mechanisms were:

- requiring the Taoiseach to prepare and publish a Green Paper on reunification within a defined timeframe;
- broad consultation, including political parties, civil society, experts and unionist/Protestant communities;
- an all-island Citizens' Assembly following the Green Paper;
- periodic reporting to the Oireachtas.

Unlike the 9 June case, this was not merely a scheduling division. The vote directly determined whether the Bill would proceed beyond Second Stage.

## Core political dispute

Sinn Féin presented the Bill as a way to move Irish-unity planning from aspiration into an organised Government process. Mary Lou McDonald argued that constitutional change should be prepared for in advance and described the Green Paper and Citizens' Assembly as mechanisms for working through practical questions.

The Government did not frame its opposition as opposition to Irish unity in principle. Taoiseach Micheál Martin argued that the proposed timetable and mechanism were not credible and that a Citizens' Assembly was not the appropriate way to conduct the preparatory work. Fine Gael figures similarly argued that constitutional change should not be driven by arbitrary deadlines.

Contemporary reporting therefore describes a substantial difference over **timing, mechanism and who should lead formal preparation**, rather than a simple binary split between people who favour or oppose a united Ireland.

## Why the vote was close

### Confirmed

- The Bill was defeated by only ten votes, 69–79.
- Sinn Féin sponsored the Bill.
- Contemporary reporting says it did not win support from Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael or independents sufficient to pass.
- The debate was explicitly about whether Government should be legally required to begin a structured Green Paper/Citizens' Assembly process on a fixed timetable.

### Externally reported

The Irish Times reported that Martin considered the 18-month Green Paper deadline not credible and viewed a Citizens' Assembly as an unsuitable mechanism for the scale of the preparatory work. BreakingNews/PA reported Simon Harris saying constitutional change could not be driven by arbitrary timelines. The Irish Times later characterised Sinn Féin and Government as operating on very different timetables for unity.

### Supported interpretation

This is the strongest of the three initial cases for a standalone post. The narrow margin reflects a meaningful parliamentary split over **whether formal state-led preparation for reunification should begin now under a statutory timetable**, while the principal Government objection centred on the proposed process/timing rather than rejection of constitutional change as a legitimate future question.

## Editorial assessment

- **Public interest:** very high.
- **Substantive significance:** very high.
- **Visual clarity:** very high.
- **Sensitivity/caveat:** describe the Bill's actual mechanisms. Do not label the division simply as “69 for a united Ireland, 79 against”, because that is not what the proposition asked.
- **Post potential:** **very high**. Strong framing: “A Bill to require formal planning for constitutional change was defeated 69–79. What exactly did it propose, and why did Government oppose it?”
