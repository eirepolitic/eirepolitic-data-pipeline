## Brief 1 — Planning for Constitutional Change Bill 2026

### Recommended headline

**A Bill to require formal planning for constitutional change lost by 10 votes**

### Core hook

On 8 July 2026 the Dáil rejected the Planning for Constitutional Change Bill by **69 Tá to 79 Níl**. The vote was not “for or against a united Ireland”: it was about whether the State should be legally required to begin a defined Green Paper and Citizens' Assembly process on a statutory timetable.

### Recommended 5-slide structure

**Slide 1 — The vote**  
`69 Tá | 79 Níl | margin 10`  
Caption line: “One of the closest recorded Dáil divisions in the current EirePolitic snapshot.”

**Slide 2 — What the Bill would have done**  
Use a simple flow:
`Government Green Paper → consultation → all-island Citizens' Assembly → Oireachtas reporting`

Explain that the Bill sought formal planning for possible constitutional change/reunification rather than calling the referendum itself.

**Slide 3 — The case for it**  
Sinn Féin's argument: preparation should begin before a referendum becomes imminent; practical questions around services, finance, governance and constitutional arrangements should be worked through publicly and in advance.

**Slide 4 — The case against it**  
Government argument: the proposed statutory timetable was not credible, a Citizens' Assembly was not the right mechanism for this work, and reconciliation / Shared Island / Good Friday Agreement processes should remain the focus rather than imposing this particular legislative timetable.

**Slide 5 — What the vote actually tells us**  
The Dáil rejected **this planning mechanism and timetable** by ten votes. It does not provide a 69–79 measure of support for Irish unity itself.

### Primary visual

A centred 69/79 vote bar with a 10-vote gap, followed by a process diagram of the Bill's proposed Green Paper → Citizens' Assembly pathway.

- exact division ID and member-level vote rows;
- exact 69–79 result;
- official Bill/debate context;
- contemporary Irish Times reporting on the Government/Sinn Féin disagreement;
- Sinn Féin's own public case for the Bill.

### Party-vote note

Do **not** publish precise party totals yet. The raw vote feed has no populated party field for this division. A research-only event-date membership join resolves 84/148 party labels but leaves 64 unresolved. The resolved rows and external reporting support the broad Government/opposition pattern, but exact party arithmetic is not yet certified.

### Caption angle

Neutral descriptive angle: explain why a Bill about unity planning could lose narrowly even though Government parties did not frame their opposition as opposition to unity in principle. The core distinction is **constitutional objective vs proposed planning process/timetable**.

### Do not say

- “The Dáil voted 79–69 against a united Ireland.”
- “Only 69 TDs support Irish unity.”
- “Fianna Fáil/Fine Gael oppose reunification” based solely on this vote.

### Editorial rating

- public interest: **very high**

- sensitivity/misframing risk: **medium-high**
- recommended for final five: **yes**

## Brief 2 — Counselling-record disclosure amendment

### Recommended headline

**A counselling-record amendment lost 68–78 — but both sides said privacy protections needed to improve**

### Core hook

The close vote on 10 June 2026 was not a binary choice between protecting counselling notes and allowing unrestricted disclosure. The dispute was over **how strict the legal test should be when counselling records are sought in sexual-offence proceedings**.

### Recommended 5-slide structure

**Slide 1 — The vote**  
`68 Tá | 78 Níl | margin 10`  
Subhead: “Amendment No. 17, Criminal Law, Civil Law and Defence (Miscellaneous Provisions) Bill 2026.”

**Slide 2 — Why counselling records matter**  
Explain that therapy/counselling records can contain deeply private material and are not created as witness statements. Existing law already provided a court process for disclosure, but the Government and opposition agreed that protections needed strengthening.

**Slide 3 — The opposition amendment**  
Alan Kelly's amendment proposed a stronger presumption against disclosure and a narrower legal route for counselling records. Other opposition amendments in the same group sought still stronger restrictions, including formulations approaching or creating an absolute ban.

**Slide 4 — The Government position**  
The Government proposed a presumption of non-disclosure plus mandatory judicial scrutiny, but retained exceptional disclosure where a judge considered it necessary for a fair trial or otherwise justified under the statutory test. The Minister cited Attorney General advice that a blanket ban would create constitutional fair-trial problems.

**Slide 5 — What the 68–78 result means**  
The amendment lost by ten votes. The division was fundamentally about **where to draw the legal boundary between therapeutic privacy and fair-trial rights**, not whether counselling records deserve protection at all.

### Primary visual

Start with the 68/78 vote bar. Then use a two-path legal-test graphic:

`Stronger/narrower disclosure threshold`  
versus  
`Presumption of non-disclosure + retained judicial discretion`

Avoid imagery that sensationalises sexual violence or victims.

### Key voices available for sourcing

- Alan Kelly — moved the compromise amendment;
- Ruth Coppinger — argued for stronger/absolute protection and highlighted the harm caused by disclosure;
- Jim O'Callaghan — defended judicial discretion and the constitutional fair-trial rationale;
- Gary Gannon — yielded time specifically so the House could reach a vote on the issue.

### External evidence available

- Department of Justice explanation of the Government proposal and Attorney General advice;
- RTÉ reporting on opposition/therapist calls for a full ban;
- Irish Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy statement on 10 June seeking absolute protections while acknowledging Government safeguards;
- later Seanad debate showing the same disagreement continued after the Dáil division.

The research-only event-date membership join resolves 86/146 rows and leaves 60 unresolved. The resolved portion shows Government parties on Níl, principal opposition parties on Tá and independents split, but this is not yet sufficient for certified party arithmetic.

### Caption angle

Neutral descriptive angle: “Both sides agreed the current treatment of counselling records needed reform. The close vote was over how far the law could restrict disclosure while protecting a defendant's fair-trial rights.”

### Do not say

- “78 TDs voted against victims' privacy.”
- “The Government voted to give counselling notes to accused people.”
- “68 TDs voted for a total ban” unless discussing the exact amendment that actually proposed one.
- any causal claim that one side's position would necessarily increase/decrease convictions unless directly attributed to a source.
