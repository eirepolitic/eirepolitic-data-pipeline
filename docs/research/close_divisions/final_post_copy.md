# Post 1 — Planning for Constitutional Change Bill 2026

## Cover

**A Bill to require formal planning for constitutional change lost by 10 votes**

**69 Tá · 79 Níl**  
Dáil Éireann · 8 July 2026

## Slide 2 — What was the vote actually about?

The Dáil was voting on Sinn Féin's **Planning for Constitutional Change Bill 2026** at Second Stage.

The Bill would have required the Government to begin a formal planning process for possible constitutional change and Irish reunification.

It was **not** a direct vote on whether Ireland should reunify.

## Slide 3 — What did the Bill propose?

The Bill proposed a defined process:

**Government Green Paper**  
↓  
**public and political consultation**  
↓  
**all-island Citizens' Assembly**  
↓  
**reporting back to the Oireachtas**

The Green Paper was intended to examine practical questions around public services, the economy, governance and the shape of a possible future united state.

## Slide 4 — Why did supporters back it?

Sinn Féin's argument was that constitutional change should be planned for **before** a referendum becomes imminent.

Mary Lou McDonald argued that nearly three decades after the Good Friday Agreement, practical questions about reunification should be examined openly and systematically rather than left until the last moment.

## Slide 5 — Why did Government oppose it?

Taoiseach Micheál Martin did not present the Government position as opposition to Irish unity in principle.

His objection was to **this mechanism and timetable**.

He argued that the proposed Green Paper deadline was not credible, that a Citizens' Assembly was not the right vehicle for the work, and that reconciliation, Shared Island cooperation and the Good Friday Agreement framework should remain central.

## Slide 6 — The result

**Tá 69**  
**Níl 79**

The Bill was defeated at Second Stage by **10 votes**.

So the safest reading of the division is:

> The Dáil narrowly rejected this proposed statutory process for planning constitutional change.

It is **not** evidence that 69 TDs supported Irish unity and 79 opposed it.

## Caption

One of the closest recorded Dáil divisions in EirePolitic's current 2026 voting snapshot came on the Planning for Constitutional Change Bill.

The Bill, introduced by Sinn Féin, would have required the Government to publish a Green Paper on reunification and establish an all-island Citizens' Assembly as part of a formal planning process.

It was defeated 69 Tá to 79 Níl.

The important distinction is what the vote does — and does not — tell us. This was not a Border Poll and it was not a direct vote on Irish unity. The disagreement was over whether this particular statutory planning process and timetable should begin now.

Sinn Féin argued that practical preparation should happen before constitutional change becomes imminent. The Government argued that the proposed timetable and Citizens' Assembly mechanism were the wrong approach, while continuing to support the Good Friday Agreement, Shared Island work and — in Government statements — the possibility of a united Ireland.

**EirePolitic metric:** recorded Tá/Níl division margin.

# Post 2 — Counselling-record disclosure amendment

## Cover

**A counselling-record amendment lost by 10 votes**

**68 Tá · 78 Níl**  
Dáil Éireann · 10 June 2026

## Slide 2 — What was being decided?

The vote came during the **Criminal Law, Civil Law and Defence (Miscellaneous Provisions) Bill 2026**.

The amendment concerned the circumstances in which private counselling records could be disclosed in sexual-offence proceedings.

Both Government and opposition speakers agreed that the existing protections needed to be strengthened.

The disagreement was over **how strict the legal test for exceptional disclosure should be**.

## Slide 3 — Why are counselling records different?

Counselling and therapy notes can contain deeply private information and are created for treatment — not as witness statements or evidence for a trial.

Opposition speakers and counselling organisations argued that disclosure can re-traumatise complainants and may discourage people from seeking therapy.

## Slide 4 — What did the opposition amendment seek?

Alan Kelly's amendment proposed a stronger presumption against disclosure and a narrower test for accessing counselling records.

Other amendments debated in the same group sought even stronger restrictions.

The broad opposition argument was that therapeutic privacy needed substantially greater protection.

## Slide 5 — What was the Government's argument?

The Government also proposed stronger protections, including a presumption of non-disclosure and judicial scrutiny.

But Minister for Justice Jim O'Callaghan argued that judges still needed limited power to order disclosure in exceptional cases — particularly where withholding material could create a real risk to an accused person's constitutional right to a fair trial.

The Government cited Attorney General advice against an absolute statutory ban.

## Slide 6 — The result

**Tá 68**  
**Níl 78**

The amendment was defeated by **10 votes**.

The safest summary is:

> The close vote was about where to draw the legal boundary between therapeutic privacy and fair-trial rights.

It was **not** a vote between one side favouring privacy and another side opposing it.

## Caption

A Dáil amendment on access to counselling records in sexual-offence proceedings was defeated by just 10 votes on 10 June 2026: 68 Tá to 78 Níl.

The underlying disagreement was more complicated than the numbers alone suggest.

Both Government and opposition speakers accepted that counselling records needed stronger protection. Opposition amendments sought a tighter legal threshold for disclosure, with some proposals going further toward an absolute ban.

The Government proposed a presumption of non-disclosure and mandatory judicial scrutiny, but argued that a judge must retain limited power to order disclosure where necessary to protect constitutional fair-trial rights.

That makes the vote a dispute over **where the law should draw the line between therapeutic privacy and a defendant's right to a fair trial**, rather than a simple vote for or against protecting counselling records.

**EirePolitic metric:** recorded Tá/Níl division margin.
