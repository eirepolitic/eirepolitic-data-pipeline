# 10 June 2026 — counselling-record disclosure amendment

## Formal vote

- **Context:** Criminal Law, Civil Law and Defence (Miscellaneous Provisions) Bill 2026, Report and Final Stages.
- **Result:** Tá 68; Níl 78; Staon 0.
- **Margin:** 10.
- **Outcome:** opposition amendment defeated.

## What was actually being voted on

The close division arose within a linked group of amendments dealing with disclosure of private third-party records in sexual-offence proceedings, especially counselling records.

Alan Kelly's amendment No. 17 proposed a stronger statutory presumption against disclosure and a tighter test for counselling records. Related opposition amendments included proposals for an absolute ban and removal of the Government wording allowing disclosure where it was otherwise 'in the interests of justice'.

The Government approach instead retained judicial gatekeeping: a judge would examine records and disclosure would be exceptional, including where non-disclosure created a real risk of an unfair trial or other specified interests-of-justice grounds applied.

## Core dispute

This was not a simple vote 'for or against victims' counselling-note privacy'. Both sides accepted a need for stronger protection. The dispute was over **how absolute the protection should be and how much discretion a court must retain to guarantee a fair trial**.

Opposition speakers and survivor/therapy advocates argued that counselling notes are exceptionally private and that disclosure itself can retraumatise complainants or deter counselling. The Government argued that an absolute statutory prohibition could conflict with an accused person's constitutional fair-trial rights, so carefully controlled judicial access had to remain possible.

## Why the vote was close

### Confirmed

- The issue had substantial cross-party concern; amendments were developed after Committee Stage and discussed as a connected package.
- The competing positions were relatively narrow in legal design: strong presumption/near-ban or ban versus strong protection with retained judicial discretion.
- The margin was ten votes.

### Externally reported / stakeholder context

The Irish Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy publicly called on the Government on 10 June for absolute protection of therapy notes. It welcomed removal of the waiver provision, tighter judicial gatekeeping and legal aid, but argued that judicial discretion did not go far enough.

Subsequent Seanad debate confirms the Government's stated design: a presumption of non-disclosure with a judge required to scrutinise the records and permit disclosure only under a restrictive legal test.

### Supported interpretation

The closeness appears to reflect a genuine policy/legal disagreement over where to place the boundary between **complainant privacy and therapeutic confidentiality** and **the constitutional right to a fair trial**, rather than disagreement over whether privacy safeguards were needed at all.
