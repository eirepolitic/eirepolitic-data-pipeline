# 10 June 2026 — counselling-record disclosure amendment

## Formal vote

- **Context:** Criminal Law, Civil Law and Defence (Miscellaneous Provisions) Bill 2026, Report and Final Stages.
- **Division:** `vote_130`.
- **Result:** Tá 68; Níl 78; Staon 0.
- **Margin:** 10.
- **Outcome:** opposition amendment No. 17 defeated.

## What was actually being voted on

The division arose within a linked group of amendments dealing with disclosure of private third-party records in sexual-offence proceedings, especially counselling records.

Alan Kelly's amendment No. 17 proposed a stronger statutory presumption against disclosure and a tighter test for counselling records. Related opposition amendments proposed still stronger protections, including an absolute ban in some formulations and removal of the Government wording permitting disclosure where it was otherwise "in the interests of justice".

The Government approach retained judicial gatekeeping. A judge would examine the records and disclosure would be exceptional, including where non-disclosure created a real risk of an unfair trial or another specified interests-of-justice ground applied.

This therefore was **not** a vote simply for or against privacy of counselling notes. Both sides accepted that the existing regime needed stronger protection. The disputed point was the legal threshold for exceptional disclosure.

## Core dispute

The debate centred on where Irish law should place the boundary between:

- **complainant privacy, therapeutic confidentiality and protection from re-traumatisation**, and
- **an accused person's constitutional right to a fair trial and access to genuinely relevant evidence**.

Opposition speakers argued that counselling notes are exceptionally private, are not created as evidentiary records and that disclosure can itself harm complainants or discourage people from seeking therapy. The Government argued that an absolute statutory prohibition could conflict with constitutional fair-trial rights and that carefully controlled judicial access therefore had to remain possible.

The Government also argued that counselling material can sometimes corroborate a complainant's account and assist a prosecution, which was part of its reason for retaining a broader judicial discretion than the opposition amendments proposed.

## Key speakers and positions

### Alan Kelly — Labour

Kelly moved amendment No. 17 and described it as a compromise drafted by Lorcan Staines SC for the Dublin Rape Crisis Centre. The proposal kept a judicial mechanism but tightened the conditions under which records could be disclosed, particularly for counselling records.

### Ruth Coppinger — People Before Profit-Solidarity

Coppinger argued for substantially stronger protection, stressing the highly personal nature of therapy records, their different purpose from witness statements and the risk of re-traumatisation. She also criticised the limited amount of parliamentary time available for such a sensitive set of amendments.

### Jim O'Callaghan — Minister for Justice, Fianna Fáil

O'Callaghan defended the Government's presumption-of-non-disclosure model but rejected an absolute ban. He relied on Attorney General advice that a blanket prohibition would create constitutional fair-trial problems, and argued that judicial scrutiny could protect privacy while allowing exceptional disclosure where necessary.

### Gary Gannon — Social Democrats

Gannon treated the issue as sufficiently important that he yielded speaking time to ensure the House could reach a division. This supports the interpretation that the vote was not a routine technical amendment but a deliberate contest over the legal threshold.

## External context

### Government position before the vote

On 4 February 2026 the Department of Justice said the Government intended to create a presumption of non-disclosure, remove the existing waiver route and require judicial scrutiny whenever counselling records were sought. The Department also said Attorney General advice was that a complete ban would be unconstitutional because of fair-trial rights.

### Opposition / professional concern

RTÉ reported earlier in 2026 that opposition TDs and therapists were calling for a full ban on the use of counselling notes in trials. On the day of the Dáil vote, the Irish Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy called for absolute protection of therapy notes, while welcoming some Government safeguards such as removal of the waiver provision and tighter judicial gatekeeping.

### Later Seanad debate

The same dispute continued in the Seanad. The Minister defended a two-stage judicial process with a presumption against disclosure, while opposition Senators continued to argue for stronger restrictions or a complete ban. This later debate corroborates that the Dáil division reflected a genuine continuing policy/legal disagreement rather than a one-off procedural split.

## Why the vote was close

### Confirmed

- The amendment lost by ten votes, 68–78.
- The competing proposals were relatively close in objective but materially different in legal threshold.
- The issue had substantial cross-party concern and had been examined by the Justice Committee.
- The recorded member coalition closely resembles a Government-versus-opposition division, with independent support split.

### Supported interpretation

The closeness is best understood as a disagreement over **how far legislation could go in protecting counselling records while remaining compatible with constitutional fair-trial requirements**. It should not be framed as one side supporting survivors and the other opposing them; both sides explicitly accepted the need to strengthen the existing protections.
