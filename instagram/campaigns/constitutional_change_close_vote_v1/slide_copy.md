## Slide 2 — What was actually being voted on?

The Dáil was voting on Sinn Féin's **Planning for Constitutional Change Bill 2026** at Second Stage.

The Bill would have required the Government to begin a formal planning process for possible constitutional change and Irish reunification.

**It was not a direct vote on whether Ireland should reunify.**

---

## Slide 3 — What did the Bill propose?

### A defined planning process

**Government Green Paper**
↓
**public and political consultation**
↓
**all-island Citizens' Assembly**
↓
**reporting to the Oireachtas**

The process was intended to examine practical questions including public services, finances, governance and possible future constitutional arrangements.

---

## Slide 4 — Why did supporters back it?

Sinn Féin argued that detailed preparation should begin **before** any unity referendum becomes imminent.

Mary Lou McDonald's case was that practical questions about constitutional change should be examined openly and systematically rather than left until the last moment.

Small source line: `Source: Dáil debate / Sinn Féin speech, 7 July 2026`

---

## Slide 5 — Why did Government oppose it?

Taoiseach Micheál Martin's objection centred on **this process and timetable**.

He argued that the proposed Green Paper deadline was not credible, that a Citizens' Assembly was not the right mechanism for the work, and that reconciliation, Shared Island cooperation and the Good Friday Agreement framework should remain central.

**Opposing this Bill was not presented as opposition to Irish unity in principle.**

Small source line: `Source: Dáil debate / contemporary Irish Times reporting`

# Caption

One of the closest recorded Dáil divisions in EirePolitic's current 2026 voting snapshot came on the Planning for Constitutional Change Bill.

The Bill, introduced by Sinn Féin, would have required the Government to publish a Green Paper on reunification and establish an all-island Citizens' Assembly as part of a formal planning process.

It was defeated **69 Tá to 79 Níl** — a margin of just 10 votes.

The important distinction is what the vote does, and does not, tell us. This was not a Border Poll and it was not a direct vote on Irish unity. The disagreement was over whether this particular statutory planning process and timetable should begin now.

Sinn Féin argued that practical preparation should happen before constitutional change becomes imminent. The Government argued that the proposed timetable and Citizens' Assembly mechanism were the wrong approach, while continuing to emphasise the Good Friday Agreement, reconciliation and Shared Island work.

**Sources:** Houses of the Oireachtas division/debate record, EirePolitic voting data and contemporary reporting.
