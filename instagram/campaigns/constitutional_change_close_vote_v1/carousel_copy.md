## Slide 2 — What was actually being voted on?

The Dáil was deciding whether the **Planning for Constitutional Change Bill 2026** should proceed beyond Second Stage.

The Bill would have required the Government to begin a formal planning process for possible constitutional change and Irish reunification.

**This was not a direct vote on Irish unity.**

## Slide 3 — What did the Bill propose?

**Government Green Paper**  
↓  
**public and political consultation**  
↓  
**all-island Citizens' Assembly**  
↓  
**reporting to the Oireachtas**

The proposed process was intended to examine practical questions before any future referendum became imminent.

## Slide 4 — Why did supporters back it?

Sinn Féin argued that constitutional change should be prepared for **before** a referendum is on the immediate horizon.

The case was that questions around public services, the economy, governance and constitutional arrangements should be examined openly and in advance.

## Slide 5 — Why did Government oppose it?

Government opposition focused on **this mechanism and timetable**.

Taoiseach Micheál Martin argued that the proposed deadline was not credible and that a Citizens' Assembly was not the right vehicle for the work.

Government instead emphasised reconciliation, Shared Island cooperation and the Good Friday Agreement framework.

## Slide 6 — What does the 69–79 vote tell us?

**Tá 69 · Níl 79**  
**Margin: 10 votes**

The Dáil narrowly rejected **this proposed statutory planning process**.

It does **not** mean:

- 69 TDs supported Irish unity;
- 79 TDs opposed Irish unity;
- the Dáil voted against reunification itself.

# Caption

One of the closest recorded Dáil divisions in EirePolitic's current 2026 voting snapshot came on the Planning for Constitutional Change Bill 2026.

The Bill would have required the Government to publish a Green Paper on possible constitutional change and establish an all-island Citizens' Assembly as part of a formal planning process.

On 8 July it was defeated at Second Stage by **69 Tá to 79 Níl** — a margin of ten votes.

The important distinction is what this vote does and does not tell us. It was not a Border Poll and it was not a direct vote on Irish unity. The disagreement was over whether this particular statutory planning process and timetable should begin now.

Sinn Féin argued that practical preparation should happen before constitutional change becomes imminent. The Government argued that the proposed timetable and Citizens' Assembly mechanism were the wrong approach, while emphasising reconciliation, Shared Island cooperation and the Good Friday Agreement framework.
